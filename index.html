Australian Public Art Collections

From Gallery to Gallery:

Australia's Visual Heritage

Comparing public gallery collections, artistic periods, artwork categories and collecting priorities to explore Australia's visual arts heritage.

Introduction

What do Australia's public art collections reveal about its visual heritage?

Public galleries help preserve Australia's visual arts heritage by collecting artworks from different periods, cultures, artistic traditions and media. Yet no single gallery represents this heritage in exactly the same way. Each institution develops its collection through different priorities and areas of specialisation.

This visualisation compares Australian public galleries through their geographic distribution, collection themes and artistic periods before examining QAGOMA as a detailed collection case study. Together, the comparisons show how Australia's visual heritage is formed through both shared collecting responsibilities and distinctive institutional choices.

Guiding question

How do different Australian public galleries collect, preserve and represent Australia's visual arts heritage?

Section 01 · Geography

Where are Australia's public art collections preserved?

Before comparing the collections themselves, it is useful to understand the gallery network that preserves them. Public galleries operate across every Australian state and territory, but their distribution is uneven, creating different levels of institutional presence across the country.

Gallery distribution

Which states have the largest public gallery networks?

Based on the Australian Public Galleries Snapshot 2019, this chart compares estimated numbers of small-to-medium public galleries across Australia's states and territories.

What this reveals

Australia's public gallery network is concentrated unevenly across states and territories.

Queensland records the largest estimated number of small-to-medium public galleries at 104, followed by New South Wales with 96 and Victoria with 78. Western Australia and South Australia also have substantial gallery networks, while the Australian Capital Territory, Tasmania and the Northern Territory record much smaller totals.

This uneven distribution matters for Australia's visual arts heritage because public galleries are the institutions that collect, preserve and exhibit artworks. States with larger gallery networks may have more institutions contributing to the preservation and public presentation of visual heritage, while smaller states and territories operate through more limited gallery networks.

Why are the gallery counts in chart 1 and 2 different?

The state comparison uses historical estimates from the Australian Public Galleries Snapshot, while the interactive map uses a separately compiled set of individual gallery locations. The two datasets therefore describe different populations and should not be compared as equivalent totals.

What this reveals

Gallery locations are concentrated around major urban and regional centres rather than distributed evenly.

In the selected location dataset, gallery records cluster strongly along Australia's more populated coastal regions, particularly around the east and south-east. When Victoria is selected, Melbourne has the highest number of recorded gallery locations, followed by smaller concentrations in places such as Parkville and Bendigo.

This pattern matters for public art collections because the location of galleries affects where audiences are most likely to encounter collected artworks. Larger urban centres can support several institutions within a relatively small area, while regional and remote communities are represented by fewer individual gallery locations.

What kind of gallery network supports Australia's public collections?

Australia's visual heritage is preserved through a broad network of cultural institutions. Understanding the scale and composition of this network provides context for the different types of collections explored later in the visualisation.

Gallery types

What types of institutions preserve public collections?

Public and regional galleries make up the largest institutional category in the surveyed sector, while contemporary art spaces, art museums, craft galleries and Aboriginal art centres form smaller groups.

A distributed collecting network

Australia's visual arts heritage is not preserved only by a small number of national institutions. Regional and public galleries form a large part of the sector, meaning public collections are maintained across many communities and geographic areas.

Different institutions, different roles

The presence of several gallery types suggests that public collection activity occurs within institutions with different purposes and audiences. This creates the foundation for different collecting priorities, themes and areas of specialisation across Australia's gallery network.

Section 03 · Collection themes & categories

What do Australian galleries choose to collect and preserve?

Comparing collection themes and artwork categories reveals how galleries construct different versions of visual heritage. Some priorities are shared across major institutions, while other collecting areas distinguish one gallery from another.

What artwork categories make up the QAGOMA Collection?

A detailed case study of one Australian public collection

This internal variation helps explain how one gallery can contain several collecting identities at once. Australia's visual heritage can therefore be organised not only by period or culture, but also through different media and departmental collecting traditions.

Australia's visual heritage is preserved through both shared themes and distinctive collection identities.

The cross-gallery comparison reveals overlap in important collecting themes, but also clear differences between institutions. The QAGOMA case study shows how those broad priorities become visible at the level of individual artwork categories and specialised collecting areas.

Section 04 · Preserving the collection

How is visual heritage preserved and made accessible?

Collection themes and artwork categories describe what galleries preserve. Stewardship determines whether those works can continue to function as part of Australia's visual heritage. Permanent collections, digitisation and online access provide different forms of long-term preservation and public visibility.

Long-term stewardship

How common are permanent collections?

The survey indicates that permanent collecting is common among public galleries. Each square represents one percentage point, comparing galleries that report holding a permanent collection with those that do not.

What this means for visual heritage

Most surveyed galleries act as long-term custodians.

Around 72% of surveyed galleries report holding a permanent collection, compared with 28% that do not. This supports the role of galleries as institutions that retain artworks and cultural material beyond individual exhibitions.

72% hold a permanent collection
Digital preservation & access

Does digitisation make public collections accessible online?

Digital documentation can support preservation and collection management, but digitised material is not necessarily made publicly available. Comparing the two measures reveals the difference between recording a collection and providing digital public access to it.

Preservation versus access

Digital preservation does not automatically equal public access.

The survey reports 74% of collection material as digitised, compared with 35% available online. The difference shows that a substantial amount of digitally recorded collection material remains unavailable through public online access.

39 percentage-point gap between digitisation and online access
The bigger picture

Preserving visual heritage and providing access to it are related, but they are not the same task.

Permanent collections establish long-term custodianship, while digitisation creates another layer of documentation. However, the access gap demonstrates that preservation alone does not guarantee that audiences can encounter collection material online.

Artwork category by period · case study

How do artistic forms differ across creation periods?

Artwork categories across creation periods in the QAGOMA Collection

This chart examines the current QAGOMA Collection by grouping artworks according to their recorded creation period and physical category. It compares how strongly different artwork forms are represented within each period.

Explore artwork forms

Select a category to follow its representation across creation periods.

The mix of artwork forms differs substantially between creation periods.

Print accounts for its highest share in works from 1950–1979, while photography has a larger share in 1980–1999 than in the earlier periods shown.

Works created between 2000 and 2024 show a different composition: sculpture has a larger share than in several earlier periods, while the combined “Other” category accounts for almost 30%. These patterns describe the composition of the collection by artwork creation period. They should not be interpreted as a historical record of how QAGOMA's collection itself changed at each point in time.

About this comparison

Artwork creation periods were derived from QAGOMA's DateCreated field. Painting, Print, Photograph, Drawing, Sculpture and Ceramic are shown separately, while remaining physical categories are grouped as “Other”. Records without a usable creation date or category were excluded.

What artistic periods tell us

Australia's visual heritage is represented through multiple generations of artistic production rather than one single period.

The acquisition comparison shows that contemporary art plays an important role in recent collecting, while historical works remain present. The QAGOMA case study further demonstrates that different periods contain different combinations of media. Together, period and category reveal two complementary dimensions of how public collections represent visual heritage.

Section 06 · Building the collection

How can one public collection bring past and present together?

A public collection is not created at one moment. It develops through repeated acquisition decisions. The QAGOMA case study examines when artworks entered the collection and how long after creation they were acquired, showing how contemporary and retrospective collecting can exist within the same institution.

Acquisition history · case study

When did artworks enter the QAGOMA Collection?

Recorded acquisitions grouped by decade

Grouping QAGOMA's recorded acquisition dates by decade reveals when the collection expanded most strongly. This is a history of acquisition activity rather than a timeline of when the artworks themselves were created.

Building a public collection

Recorded acquisition activity expanded strongly from the late twentieth century.

Acquisition totals increase substantially from the 1970s onward and reach their highest recorded decade in the 2010s. This demonstrates how a public collection can accumulate progressively over many decades.

The result also helps distinguish two ideas: an artwork's creation period and the date on which a gallery chooses to acquire it. A gallery can therefore expand a collection today while collecting work from either the present or much earlier artistic periods.

The 2020s figure covers acquisitions only through December 2024 and therefore represents an incomplete decade. It should not be interpreted as evidence of a full-decade decline.

Creation to acquisition · case study

How soon after creation do artworks enter the collection?

Time between recorded creation year and acquisition

Comparing creation year with acquisition year distinguishes works collected relatively close to the time of production from works acquired retrospectively many years later.

Contemporary and retrospective collecting

The collection combines recently produced works with artworks acquired much later.

Around 41.8% of artworks with usable dates entered the collection within five years of their recorded creation year. This indicates a substantial connection between acquisition activity and relatively recent artistic production.

At the same time, approximately 24.7% were acquired more than fifty years after creation. Retrospective acquisitions allow earlier artistic production to become part of a collection even when those works were not collected close to the time they were made.

About this analysis

Acquisition year was derived from QAGOMA's AcquiredDate field and compared with the first usable year recorded in DateCreated. Records without both usable dates were excluded.

What collection growth reveals

Visual heritage can be built through both immediate and retrospective collecting.

The QAGOMA case study shows that a public collection can represent different periods not only because works were produced at different times, but also because galleries can acquire older works long after their creation.

How to read this choropleth

The values shown on this map should not be interpreted as an absolute measure of a country's artistic importance or influence. Instead, the map represents how strongly each country appears within the QAGOMA Artists and Organisations dataset.

The choropleth itself uses the number of recorded artist associations to determine colour intensity. Countries with more recorded artists appear darker, while countries with fewer recorded artists appear lighter.

Conclusion · Australia's visual arts heritage

Australia's visual arts heritage is preserved collectively, but represented differently across public galleries.

Across the visualisation, the charts show that Australia's public gallery network is geographically widespread but unevenly distributed. These institutions support the preservation of visual heritage through permanent collections, collection management and increasing levels of digitisation and public access.

More importantly, comparisons between major galleries reveal that their collections are not identical. Shared themes such as Australian and First Nations art appear across several institutions, while other collecting areas vary. Artistic-period comparisons also show different balances between historical and contemporary works, demonstrating how each gallery develops its own collecting priorities.

The QAGOMA case study adds further detail by showing how artwork categories, collecting departments, acquisition timing and artist geographic associations contribute to the structure of one public collection. Together, these patterns show that Australia's visual arts heritage is not represented through one single collection or timeline, but through multiple galleries whose different themes, periods, media and collecting decisions preserve complementary parts of Australia's artistic record.
